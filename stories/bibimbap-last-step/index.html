Who gets the last job in a bibimbap kitchen?

2026-11-23

The kitchen arranges the toppings so carefully. Then someone hands you a spoon. How much of the red paste should go in?

A bowl arrives with little piles of green spinach, pale bean sprouts and strips of beef arranged around the center. Someone at the next table takes a photo. A moment later their spoon goes straight through the arrangement. There is rice underneath, and the name of the dish gives away who has the last job in this kitchen. You do.

비비다 (bibida) means to mix, and 밥 (bap) is cooked rice. Before that final stir, a cook may have prepared each topping separately. Think of a bean sprout that still crunches beside a piece of soft spinach. A good spoonful lets you taste both. Look closely at a Jeonju-style bowl and you might spot something yellow that looks a little like egg. It could be yellow mung bean jelly, a traditional ingredient in the city's version.

If you're curious about the red paste, ask for it on the side when you order. 고추장은 따로 주세요 (gochujangeun ttaro juseyo) gives the cook a clear request. Add a little gochujang, pull some rice up from the bottom with your spoon and taste. It's much easier to put another dab in than to fish the first one back out.

Eating with a friend gives you another line to try. Lift your spoon and ask 이렇게 비비면 돼요? (ireoke bibimyeon dwaeyo), meaning “Do I mix it like this?” See what your friend does next. They might show you their favorite way to mix it or confess that they always take a bite of the egg first. Which ingredient would you want in your very first spoonful?
