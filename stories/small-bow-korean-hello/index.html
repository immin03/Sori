Open Korea 101 in Sori

The smallest part of a Korean hello.

A slight bow can say a lot before the conversation begins.

Imagine meeting a friend’s parent for the first time in Korea. You have practised your greeting, but suddenly there is another question: what should your body do? Before you can decide, they offer a small bow. The whole movement takes less time than your carefully rehearsed sentence.

In many everyday greetings, a Korean bow can be a slight dip of the head and upper body. It accompanies the words rather than stopping the conversation for a ceremony. Think of it as a quiet way to acknowledge the person in front of you.

The setting matters. A greeting to an older person or someone in a senior role may be more formal than one between close friends. There is no single movement that fits every relationship. For an ordinary introduction, a small, unhurried bow gives you a place to start; you can take your cue from the people around you.

Try pairing that first introduction with 만나서 반가워요. It means “Nice to meet you.” Say it slowly enough to enjoy saying it. You are welcoming a person into a conversation, so leave a little space for their name and their reply.

Sometimes a handshake follows. If the other person offers one, you can use your right hand and lightly support it with your left. You do not need to rush through a bow and a handshake at the same moment. A brief pause makes room to notice what the other person is doing.

Bows can also appear when people say goodbye. Once you start noticing them, the interesting part is how small some are: a short movement that helps close an encounter. You do not need to greet every passerby or turn each interaction into a formal occasion.

On your next introduction, give your hello a little room. Put the phone down, notice the person and let the words arrive with the movement. What small gesture makes a greeting feel warm where you live?
