Why is there a basket of lettuce at Korean barbecue?

2026-11-23

One leaf, a little rice and a piece of pork. Learn how to make a ssam, offer one to someone else and negotiate the last bite in Korean.

Someone puts a basket of lettuce beside the grill. You reach for a leaf, then pause. There is no salad bowl. Across the table, your friend has a leaf open on her palm. She adds a piece of pork, barely a spoonful of rice and a dab of reddish paste. She folds the edges in and eats the whole thing. By the time you have decided what to put in yours, she is already making another.

Welcome to Korea 101. Today we’re pulling up a chair at a Korean barbecue table. We’ll make one very small dinner decision together: what goes into a single leaf? Along the way, you’ll learn how to ask a friend to show you, how to offer someone a bite, and what to say when the last piece of pork is still sitting on the grill.

The little parcel is a 쌈 (ssam). The verb 싸다 (ssada) means “to wrap.” You can hear the same verb when someone talks about wrapping a present. At dinner, though, the object might be a slippery piece of grilled pork belly. If you are making your first ssam, try a leaf about the size of your palm. Put the meat in the middle, fold the bottom edge up and bring the sides together. If the first one falls apart, eat what you have and try a smaller filling on the next round.

Now try two with exactly the same pork. Make the first with lettuce, 상추 (sangchu). For the second, pick a 깻잎 (kkaennip), a perilla leaf. The lettuce gives a cool crunch and then gets out of the way. The perilla has a scent that stays with you after the meat is gone. You may understand why the person beside you has been carefully peeling those leaves apart all evening. Or you may go straight back to the lettuce. Either way, you’ve learned something about your own order.

If your introduction to ssam was a barbecue restaurant, it is easy to imagine that grilled meat came first and someone invented a leafy wrapper to go with it. A cookbook published in 1924 offers a different picture. As the National Folk Museum of Korea notes, it described napa cabbage as a common wrapper. You could bring leaves and cooked rice to a table and already have the beginnings of a meal.

That older idea is still right there on a restaurant sign. Look for 쌈밥 (ssambap). You already know 쌈, and 밥 is cooked rice. At a ssambap restaurant, the selection of leaves can be worth paying attention to in its own right. One might be crisp and raw, another cabbage leaf tender enough to fold over with a fingertip. The Korea Tourism Organization describes versions with fish as well as meat. A piece of fish tucked into soft cabbage makes a very different lunch from pork hot off the grill.

Back at our barbecue table, the reddish-brown paste is 쌈장 (ssamjang). It is usually made with 된장 (doenjang, fermented soybean paste) and 고추장 (gochujang, red pepper paste). Recipes vary. Start with a little on the tip of a chopstick. That leaves room to taste the pork, and you can always add more to the next bite. Garlic, sliced chillies or kimchi might be within reach too. You can leave every one of them on its plate.

There is a practical reason to start small. Once your friend has folded a generous leaf around two pieces of pork, rice and a whole garlic clove, that is exactly when someone will ask her a question. Give her a moment. A nod and a laugh are usually all she can manage. When it is your turn to talk, try 이거 어떻게 싸 먹어요? (igeo eotteoke ssa meogeoyo?), “How do you wrap this to eat it?” Someone can answer that question with their hands even if you don’t catch every word.

Suppose your friend sets a finished wrap on your plate. She remembered you skipped the raw garlic last time. Without saying much, she has made something that is yours down to the last ingredient. If you’d like to return the gesture, ask 쌈 하나 싸 드릴까요? (ssam hana ssa deurilkkayo?), “Shall I make you a wrap?” The ending 드릴까요 makes the offer polite. You can use the same ending when offering to pour someone water. Pause for the answer, especially if you’ve only just met.

Later, there is one piece of pork left. Everyone can see it. If you want to offer it around, 마지막 한 점 드실래요? (majimak han jeom deusillaeyo?) means “Would you like the last piece?” Listen for 점 (jeom). It is a handy word for a piece of meat or fish. A friend might say 반씩 먹어요 (banssik meogeoyo), “Let’s have half each.” If someone offers it to you and you want it, 네, 잘 먹을게요 (ne, jal meogeulgeyo), “Yes, thank you, I’ll enjoy it,” gives them an answer and lets dinner carry on.

You can practise this at home with one lettuce leaf and a little leftover rice. Say 쌈 as you pick up the leaf. Ask someone whether they’d like one before you start choosing their filling. Then let them make one for you. Even people eating from exactly the same dishes rarely end up with the same bite.

If someone at your table offered to make you a ssam tonight, what would you ask them to put inside? And is there anything you would quietly ask them to leave out?
